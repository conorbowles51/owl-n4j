## 3. Studies of examiners, practitioners and analysts

**Examiner reliability and bias** (S5) [peer-reviewed, read through a secondary report]. 53 digital forensic
examiners from 8 countries analysed the same drive for 11 traces. None found all 11; 8 percent found 8 to 10.
Examiners given a context suggesting the suspect might be innocent found the fewest traces. The study was
published online on 24 May 2021. Bearing on spec 07: model readings are marked until a person confirms them,
examples cover every kind before a confirmation (07 4.11, J-8), and coverage of what was and was not examined
is recorded (07 J-13).

**German judicial users of digital forensics** (S3) [peer-reviewed venue]. A survey of 101 judicial
practitioners: 15 judges, 46 prosecutors, 7 digital-forensic experts, 12 investigators, 10 executives, 8 admin
or IT staff and 3 others. Respondents asked for better integration of digital forensics into their workflows,
better cross-domain communication and "easier and faster access to evidential data and results". 3 of the 15
judges (20 percent) had digital-evidence training (section 4.1). Its section 2.2 cites S9: users "need tools
that require less cognitive effort, support collaborative work environments, and have high interoperability".

**Competing hypotheses and confirmation bias** (S4) [peer-reviewed]. Study 1 had 161 participants; study 2 had
62 Dutch military analysts. Participants shown hypotheses in rows and evidence in columns showed significantly
less confirmation bias. The layout of the analysis-of-competing-hypotheses matrix (hypotheses in columns) and a
paragraph format did not reduce it, and the matrix gave no gain in sensitivity to evidence credibility. Most of
the 62 analysts showed no confirmation bias. Bearing on spec 07: UXD-27 keeps a per-theory Supports and
Contradicts view rather than a full evidence-by-hypothesis matrix.

**Triage in English police forces** (S6) [peer-reviewed, read through the university's release]. Based on 120
hours of ethnographic observation and 43 interviews in four in-house digital forensic laboratories. Triage
reduced the items sent to laboratories, but submissions to triage stayed high and backlogs persisted. The
journal volume and pages, 43(1) 77 to 90 (not verified), come from search results only.

**Scotland's kiosks** (Q20) [practice]. Cyber kiosks are used "solely to provide an initial triage capability",
which "can negate the need for full digital forensic examination" at a hub (paragraph 1.2).

**Vendor surveys** [vendor, commercial interest]. S7: investigators spend about 69 hours per case on
multi-device review (analysts about 79); 68 percent of investigators do not have enough time to review data
fully; 8 in 10 report lacking proper investigative tools; over 2,100 respondents. S8: an earlier survey of
about 2,700 law enforcement staff in more than 20 countries found most agencies review digital data manually,
and only 24 percent of North American agencies used an analytics tool on mobile photos and videos.

## 4. Usability method

| Point | Figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Small qualitative rounds | testing with 5 users finds almost as many problems as many more users (N1); 5 users found 85 percent of the problems in the model behind that advice (N2) | N1, N2 |
| Quantitative studies | about 20 users, about 4 times the cost of a qualitative round | N3 |
| Response-time limits | 0.1 s feels instant; 1.0 s keeps the flow of thought; 10 s is about the limit of attention; longer waits need a percent-done indicator and the expected completion | N4 (Usability Engineering, 1993) |
| System Usability Scale | 10 items, scored 0 to 100; the average over 500 studies is 68; created by Brooke in 1986, and the 1996 publication year cited in spec 07 (not verified) | N5 |
| One-question post-task rating | CHI 2009 compared three one-question types (Likert, usability magnitude estimation, subjective mental effort) with 26 participants; the Likert and subjective-mental-effort types were more sensitive with small samples; the 7-point Single Ease Question performs about as well as or better than the more complicated measures; above 10 to 12 participants any of the three gives reliable results | N6, N7 |

Spec 07 section 10.3 uses rounds of up to 5 participants, the single-ease rating after each task and SUS at the
end, and labels results from fewer than about 20 people as directional; these rows are the basis.

## 6. What the sources do not support

- S2 does not report SUS scores; the published SUS means for phone-review tools are S1's.
- S4 does not show that a hypothesis matrix fails to reduce confirmation bias: one matrix layout reduced it and
  the competing-hypotheses layout did not.
- N6 compares three one-question ratings with each other, not one question against longer questionnaires.
- N1 states no percentage; the 85 percent figure is N2's.
- Q12 does not tell examiners to validate device timestamps with carrier records; it uses network records to
  check locations and treats time differences as expected.
- Q20 does not say kiosks come before a full examination; it says kiosk triage can remove the need for one.
- Q5 says the term "Full" extraction should be avoided, not full extraction itself.
- P2 does not describe Portable Case as read-only; it allows comments.
- P5 names no separate layouts for quality control, issue coding or the privilege log.
- P14 does not mention import specifications for call records or phone extractions; P18 names no map feature.
- P25's README does not list saved views, stories or labels; P27 does not list multi-device timelines,
  communication graphs or map export.
