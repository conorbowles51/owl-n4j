# What three markets need, and what three platforms do

Loupe, Maltego and Kaseware against criminal defence, corporate intelligence, and litigation
support and disputes. Capability findings are drawn from published product documentation, release
notes and pricing, current to 3 August 2026.

---

## The three markets

### Criminal defence

You receive the evidence. The prosecution hands you discovery — phone extractions, documents, bank
records, bodycam, jail calls — and you did not collect any of it and cannot. The job is to know that
material better than the other side, and to find what breaks their theory. Volumes are punishing:
ten phones, six-figure document counts, court deadlines. Every assertion you make gets challenged,
so every assertion has to point at a page. And what wins is frequently what is *absent* — the
message that puts someone somewhere else, the date that does not line up.

**What this buyer needs.** Everything held in one model, so a person is the same person across the
phone, the documents and the bank statements. The phone extraction treated as real records —
individual calls and messages you can query — rather than a spreadsheet dump. A chronology that
survives a courtroom. Every fact carrying its source. And something that actively hunts
contradictions.

**What this buyer does not need.** To collect anything from the open web. To produce documents to
another party. Machinery for batching a review team. These are genuinely irrelevant here, not
weaknesses to excuse.

### Corporate intelligence and business investigations

A client or their counsel asks a question: is this counterparty what they claim, where did the money
go, who really owns this, was there fraud. The engagements are asset tracing, fraud and corruption,
due diligence and disputes intelligence. The process is consistent across the field — scope it, plan
your sources, collect, verify, analyse, write the report — and the report has to survive court.

**What this buyer needs, in two distinct halves.** At the front: reach outside the engagement, into
corporate registries, beneficial ownership, sanctions and PEP lists, court filings, property deeds
and press archives — and the ability to start from a name and pivot outward. At the back: take all
of that plus whatever the client handed over, corroborate every finding against independent sources,
hunt the inconsistencies and red flags, and produce a report where each claim is sourced. Bank
records are the starting point of nearly every asset trace.

**Why the split matters commercially.** The front is where the market's money already goes, to data
vendors the firm licenses anyway. The back is where the billable analyst hours go — and those hours
are currently spent in Excel and Word. Junior consultants at these firms bill $300–$500 an hour;
principals bill $800–$1,500. A six-week engagement with a team of four generates $150,000–$250,000
in fees.

### Litigation support and disputes

Forensic accounting, fraud claims, asset tracing for enforcement, contentious insolvency, damages.
You serve law firms, and your output is an expert report that gets cross-examined.

**What this buyer needs.** Everything the back half of corporate intelligence needs, held to a
higher standard because someone will attack your method in a hearing. On top of that, things defence
work never asks for: producing material to another party — redaction, privilege logs, numbered
exhibits, load files — batching a review team through a large disclosure set, and being able to show
your parsing has been independently validated. And because the client is a law firm, the
firm-maturity requirements are real: proven at volume, plugs into the systems the firm already runs,
and will still exist in three years.

**One distinction that decides everything here.** The industry that calls itself litigation support
is court reporting, interpreting, record retrieval, transcription and trial graphics — bought by
legal assistants and paralegals, won on responsiveness, serving personal injury, insurance and
medical malpractice matters. That is a logistics and services business, and a rear-end collision
file contains no evidence-modelling problem. The tier described above — disputes investigation and
forensic accounting — is a different business wearing the same name.

## The three platforms

### Loupe

**What it does.** It takes everything that arrives in a matter — phone extractions, documents, bank
statements, audio, video, images — and resolves it into a single model where the same person is the
same object across all of it.

Underneath that: a phone extraction becomes first-class structured records, so individual calls,
messages, contacts and locations are each queryable. Every extracted fact carries its verbatim
quote, page and file, stored with the fact. Questions whose answer depends on relationships get
answered across everything ingested, with a definite result — not a pile of documents that looked
relevant. Names and places spoken in audio or shown in video become the same objects as the ones in
documents. Bank records get categorised, counterparties resolved to who they actually are, and flows
traced between parties. Chronologies persist as work product with their citations intact, as do an
investigator's own conclusions — curated collections, approved facts, built narratives. It hunts for
what contradicts a theory. Its answers are cited and its AI output can be audited. It runs
single-tenant or on-premises, so evidence never sits in a shared system.

**What it cannot do.** Reach outside the matter at all — no registries, no sanctions lists, no
adverse media, no court records. Connect to anything else — no document management, no practice
management, no legal research, no third-party data. Collect evidence at source. Produce to another
party. Run a review team. Its file-format range is narrower than the processing specialists', its
case-management workflow is thin, and it is a young company with no audited security credentials and
no published volume record.

### Maltego

**What it does.** Reach, and it is the best in the field at it. Around 120 integrated data
providers, metered by credits. Start from a name, email, domain, phone number or crypto wallet and
pivot outward to whatever relates to it. Mature visual link analysis for exploring those
connections. Real-time monitoring of social and news streams. Genuinely good preservation of what it
finds on the open web — automated screenshots, chain-of-custody logging, defensible capture of
public profiles and pages. It integrates into things, including letting a customer expose their own
proprietary data through an on-premises server. It is an established, security-certified company
with hundreds of thousands of users, and it can be bought on a card from €3,000 a year.

**What it cannot do.** Ingest the matter. Import is CSV and Excel — you map columns to entities. A
discovery PDF has to be processed into a table outside the product first, and for a phone extraction
there is no path at all. Its graph is an analyst's working document rather than a persistent model
of the case: it holds what the analyst pulled, and the underlying data stays back in the sources. It
has no real financial-records capability. Its AI reads and reports on the graph, and whether it
cites sources is not documented anywhere. There are no narrative or hypothesis constructs and no
contradiction hunting; its case feature is encrypted storage with collaboration — a container, not a
work product. Law firms and defence practices are not a named customer type anywhere in its
materials.

### Kaseware

**What it does.** It runs the investigation. Built by former FBI Special Agents who built Sentinel,
the FBI's own case management system, and it shows: intake, incident management, records, document
and evidence management, task management and assignment, dispatch, a public portal, administrative
controls, reporting and dashboards. The case exists as a managed object with a lifecycle, which is
the thing an investigating unit actually needs and the thing most analytical tools do not have.

Around that sits real analytical tooling rather than workflow alone — link analysis, timelines,
entity recognition and geospatial analysis. Collaboration and permissions are a strength:
compartmentalised access, need-to-know segregation and user auditing. There is a mobile app, and
APIs and integrations. It is SOC 2, CJIS and TX-RAMP certified, and sold to law enforcement, fusion
centres, intelligence agencies, corporate security, finance, aviation, energy and pharmaceutical
customers.

**What it cannot do.** Build the evidence model. Documents and evidence are *managed* — stored,
tracked, permissioned — not modelled, and there is no path from a discovery PDF to structured facts.
Phone extractions are not first-class records. It has no financial-records capability worth the name.
Facts do not carry their quote and page, so there is no per-claim provenance to point at under
challenge. Its AI is published as "AI Services" with no documented detail, and nothing establishes
that its answers cite sources. There are no hypothesis constructs and no contradiction hunting, and
its durable work-product objects are thin. Its public materials do not describe what data formats it
ingests at all. Law firms and defence practices are not among its named customer types.

## Who fits where

### The structural picture

The three platforms are strong at three different things, and the pattern is clean enough to be
worth stating on its own:

- **Kaseware runs the unit** — workflow, permissions, certifications, institutional maturity.
- **Maltego reaches outward** — external data, pivoting, capture of what it finds.
- **Loupe understands the material** — one model, provenance, chronology, contradiction.

Neither of the other two does what Loupe does, and **Loupe's two weakest areas are precisely what
they are respectively good at.** That is the honest shape of the field, and it is more useful than a
ranking, because it says what to build, what to integrate, and what to leave alone.

### Criminal defence

**Loupe fits, and the other two are not in this market.** What the buyer needs and what Loupe does
are the same list — it satisfies every one of the twelve requirements this buyer has.

Maltego satisfies none of them. The evidence arrives from the prosecution and there is no way to get
it into the product. Kaseware satisfies two, and is a case-management system for an investigating
agency rather than an evidence platform for the receiving side — it is sold primarily to the other
side of the table.

### Corporate intelligence

**This market splits cleanly down the middle of the engagement, and all three platforms sit in
different parts of it.** Maltego owns the sourcing and collecting. Kaseware owns running the
engagement — genuinely useful here, because this market does need case workflow, need-to-know
collaboration and enterprise-grade credentials, and it is stronger on all three than Loupe is.
Loupe owns the corroborating, analysing and reporting.

The halves are worth different amounts. The front is spend that already goes to licensed data
vendors and is not really available to compete for — winning it means becoming a data business. The
back is billable hours sitting in spreadsheets. Loupe's missing piece is reach, which can be
integrated. Maltego's missing piece is the evidence model, which is architecture. Kaseware's missing
piece is also the evidence model.

Of the nineteen things this buyer needs, Loupe meets twelve, Kaseware nine and Maltego seven — and
the ones Loupe misses are reach, integrations, workflow and company maturity, not analysis.

### Litigation support and disputes

**The industry as it defines itself is not addressable, and should not be claimed.** Neither is
eDiscovery and managed review: it is the fastest-growing thing in the vicinity and precisely the
capability set Loupe is weakest at — no batching, no coding panels, no reviewer QC, no redaction, no
privilege logs, no production formats. The incumbents own it outright.

The disputes-investigation tier is real, and it is the weakest of the three markets for all three
platforms. Loupe brings the analysis and the provenance the expert report is built on, and is missing
production mechanics, review machinery and track record — maturity items rather than architectural
ones, and most already on the roadmap. Kaseware and Maltego are both further off, for the same
reason in each case: neither can ingest the matter, and neither handles the financial records that
disputes work actually runs on.

The right framing is that these firms serve law firms, so selling to them is selling *through* them.
That makes it a channel worth raising in a meeting, and not worth a slide.

## What is safe to say

### The claim that carries all three markets

One finding does more work than everything else, and it is the one that survives being checked: **of
the platforms examined, not one holds device data, financial records and documents in a single
model.** That is not an artefact of how the comparison was drawn — it is structural. The companies
that can model evidence sell their analytical tiers to law enforcement and intelligence, or they
model devices and nothing else. The companies that will sell to this buyer are excellent at
documents and treat a phone extraction as an attachment. Modelling evidence and serving this buyer
are different engineering problems sold to different customers, so nobody has had a commercial
reason to do both.

Underneath it sits the thing no competitor can contest: this was built because real casework needed
it and nothing trialled was fit for purpose. Say "a number of platforms" and name none of them —
naming one invites a comparison on their terms, and the audience almost certainly does not know them
anyway.

### Criminal defence — claim it fully

The only market where the product is complete as it stands, with no roadmap gap between what the
buyer needs and what exists.

Safe without hedging: every feature came from a working investigator on live federal casework; cases
have been worked to completion in the platform; three attorneys on those cases have access. This
buyer receives evidence, cannot collect it, and cannot buy the analytics the other side runs on.

Not safe: that anything is signed, that it is proven at volume, or that it holds audited security
credentials. The honest framing of the security gap is that it blocks the first external customer
rather than making the product a bad fit — and it is the specific thing funding pays for. Say that
plainly rather than letting someone find it.

### Corporate intelligence — claim half of it

Safe: the corroborate–analyse–report half, which is where the billable hours sit, at $300–$500 an
hour, currently going into Excel and Word. It is the same product as the beachhead, so pursuing it
costs no roadmap.

Not safe: any open-source or external-data capability, because there is none. That means due
diligence and KYC are not addressable — that half runs on licensed data the firms already buy, and
competing there means becoming a different company. Conceding it early makes the half that is
claimed more credible.

Two precision points. **"Corporate intelligence" means the investigative services firm** — the
boutique or major-practice team doing fraud, corruption and asset tracing — not the in-house
corporate investigations function, which is a workflow-and-procurement buyer and a materially worse
fit. And **do not cite the EU due diligence directive as a tailwind**: vendor market reports do, and
it has been pushed to 2028 transposition and 2029 application with narrowed scope. It fails the
first check anyone runs.

### Litigation support — sell through it, not to it

Claim the disputes-investigation tier and the channel. Do not claim the litigation support industry,
and do not claim eDiscovery or managed review.

The demand driver worth citing is litigation funding: $29.2 billion in 2026, with 42% of commercial
disputes now carrying third-party funding. A funder is a cost-disciplined buyer with a direct
interest in reducing the hours a matter consumes, which makes it the most receptive audience a
labour-substitution argument can have.

### On competitors, in any market

Answer with the category and that vendor's own published limit, not the name. An audience that has
not heard of a vendor learns nothing from the name and everything from the limit. Where a competitor
is genuinely strong, say so — the complement is more credible than the contest, and in two of these
three cases it is also true.

### Two regulatory facts worth more than any market-size number

Both are published by institutions rather than vendors, both are dateable, and neither requires the
audience to have heard of a single competitor.

**Proposed Federal Rule of Evidence 707.** The Judicial Conference has recommended subjecting
machine-generated evidence to the same reliability standard as expert testimony — the proponent must
show the output rests on sufficient facts and data, produced by reliable methods, reliably applied.
Public comment closed 16 February 2026; if approved it takes effect 1 December 2027.

Two limits, and they must be stated before this is used anywhere. The rule is not law: it has
cleared comment, not the Standing Committee, Judicial Conference, Supreme Court and Congress. And it
bites only where machine output is offered *without* an expert witness — in defence and disputes
work there is usually a human testifying to the analysis, in which case Rule 702 already governs.
So this is a direction-of-travel argument, not a compliance mandate, and claiming otherwise fails on
contact with any litigator. Used correctly it is still strong: the courts are moving to make
AI-derived findings challengeable on method, which rewards an architecture where every claim carries
its verbatim quote, page and source, and makes a platform that cannot show its method a liability.

**Practice Direction 57AD.** Permanent in the Business and Property Courts of England and Wales
since 1 October 2022. Extended Disclosure runs on models — known adverse documents only, key
documents relied on plus known adverse, or particular documents and narrow classes — with the court
holding parties to burden and cost being reasonable and proportionate. A regime built around
targeted, proportionate, issue-led disclosure favours a platform that can find the specific document
and prove where it came from, over one that reviews everything.
