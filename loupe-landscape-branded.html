01 — Executive finding

The differentiator was never the citation. It is the grounding.

Between November 2025 and June 2026 every major platform shipped cited AI answers and most made them free. That was never the claim worth making. The claim is what sits underneath it: a graph of the whole case, so a question is answered by traversal rather than by a model reading documents one at a time and hoping.

The research set out to map competitors and find a beachhead. It found something more useful and less comfortable, and it forces a correction to how the product has been described.

We are not claiming that nobody else can produce a citation. Everyone can, they are good at it, and most of them now give it away. Relativity's aiR refuses to emit a fact it cannot match to source text. Everlaw's Deep Dive answers "insufficient evidence" rather than confabulate. Relativity folded aiR into the base per-gigabyte rate in November 2025, Everlaw bundled three AI features into core pricing the same month, DISCO announced agentic AI at no additional cost in February 2026, and Reveal gave its review engine away through all of 2025. Pricing power has moved off the citation layer entirely, and any pitch that leads with provenance is arguing about a free feature.

The claim is about what the AI is standing on. Every competitor in this document answers questions the same way: retrieve some passages, put them in a context window, ask a model to reason. That is a black box in the middle of the answer, and it is bounded by whatever retrieval happened to surface. It works a document at a time and it degrades as the corpus grows — which is why the published limits are so unflattering. Relativity's conversational layer caps at 300,000 documents per index and its fact extraction runs 5,000 documents per job. CoCounsel handles roughly 200 documents per comparison run with quality explicitly degrading at volume. Reveal's own documentation states that Ask is "designed for precision, not recall" and cannot find all instances of anything. Harvey runs a prompt per document across a vault and aggregates the results.

Loupe inverts the order. The evidence is resolved into a graph first — people, devices, accounts, events, transfers, documents and the relationships between them, each carrying its source. A question is then answered by traversing that model rather than sampling it. "Who was in contact with this person in the fortnight before that transfer" has a definite answer across the entire corpus, and the answer is the actual matching set, not whatever a retriever ranked highly. The citation falls out of the structure, because every node already knows where it came from. Concrete grounding is what makes a whole-case question reliable; the citation is a consequence of it, not the feature.

Two further corrections. AI built for criminal defence is no longer an empty sentence — four funded companies now occupy it: TrialKit ($4.25M seed, 20+ defence firms, LA County Public Defender), Matey/CrimD ($7.5M seed, NACDL affinity partner, already holding SOC 2 Type II and ISO 27001), JusticeText ($4.0M ARR, 4,100+ attorneys, seven statewide public-defender systems), and Longeye ($5M from a16z, giving the product to public defenders free). Loupe is late to the category label — though none of the four has a graph, and all four answer by retrieval.

And the evidence that would populate such a graph is being discarded before it arrives. Across all forty-seven platforms, not one models a phone call, a bank transfer and a document passage as citable objects in a single case graph. Be precise about what this does and does not claim: graph is not the differentiator. Cellebrite Pathfinder genuinely builds one, and Kaseware and Altia both ship link analysis and entity recognition. What none of them holds is documents and financial records in the same model as the device data. The claim is cross-evidence-type, never graph. The eDiscovery majors ingest Cellebrite extractions and then flatten calls, contacts, locations, web history and installed apps into Excel spreadsheets in an "Other Data" folder; Relativity's own processing documentation confirms call logs emerge "only as call log data in Excel format, not as individual records," and that multi-device extractions are unsupported. Everlaw splits conversations every thousand messages; Reveal slices them into 24-hour blocks. i2 removed native UFED import at version 9.1.0 and now tells customers to export CSV. Harvey, Hebbia, CoCounsel, Clearbrief and CaseMap have no phone-extraction support at all.

The claim that survives

Loupe is the only platform where a call at 02:14, a transfer of $8,400 and a line in a subpoena return are the same kind of object — resolved into one graph, each citable back to the artifact it came from. Every competitor does one of those three well and treats the other two as attachments.

That model is what makes the question answerable. A whole-case question resolves as a traversal with a definite answer, not as a retrieval whose completeness nobody can vouch for. It is the difference between "here are the passages that looked relevant" and "here is every contact between these two people in that fortnight, and here is where each one came from."

The shape of the problem

Scored across all thirty axes, nine platforms finish above Loupe — and the way they get there is the finding. Loupe scores 55 of 60 on evidence modelling and 33 of 90 on readiness; Palantir, first overall on 201, scores 40 and 64. Relativity is 20 and 63. That inversion is the honest summary of the position: the product is ahead of the company. Everything Loupe lacks is purchasable with time and money, which is the good news, and none of it is purchasable quickly, which is the bad.

Of those nine, seven cannot be sold to a defence practice at all — they fail the accessibility or deployment gate outright. The two that clear every defence gate, Magnet Axiom and Exterro FTK, are device-by-device examiner tooling with no cross-evidence model.

The uncomfortable finding

Matey — selling to the same buyer, in the same country, with the same provenance pitch — holds SOC 2 Type II and ISO 27001 today. Loupe holds neither. In this segment that is not a procurement detail; it is the first question a firm's counsel asks, and it gates the pilot.

02 — Fit and route to market

Sell to the people who receive evidence they cannot analyse

The rest of this document is evidence. This section is the conclusion drawn from it: who to sell to first, why them rather than the obvious alternatives, how to reach them, what to charge, and what has to be fixed before any of it works.

The buyer

Defence-side private investigators and criminal-defence boutiques — firms of roughly five to fifty people running federal or serious state matters. Not public defenders, not police, not corporate legal, not AmLaw. The defining characteristic is not size or sector: it is that this buyer receives evidence rather than collecting it, receives it in formats built by the other side, and has no way to analyse it properly.

That single fact produces the whole opportunity. A defence team is handed a UFDR extraction, a disclosure bundle, subpoena returns and bank records, and the tools that could make sense of that combination are either sold exclusively to law enforcement, priced for enterprises, or built to cull documents rather than model a case. Twenty-three of the forty-seven platforms here are structurally unavailable to them — set the Market Fit Lens to criminal defence and watch half the market grey out.

How to reach them

This segment has no procurement department and does not read analyst reports, which cuts both ways: nobody can block you, and nobody is looking for you. Three routes, in order of proven effectiveness:

The defence bar, because it demonstrably works. Matey went from a seed round to national presence by becoming a NACDL affinity partner and the exclusive AI eDiscovery partner of a state defence-lawyers' association. That is a repeatable, published playbook: the bar associations are the channel for this segment, they are approachable, and the endorsement carries more weight with a fifteen-person practice than any marketing spend. This should be the first commercial action taken.

Casework referral from the practice that already runs Loupe. The platform was built inside a working private-investigations firm and has carried federal matters to trial. Every attorney on the other side of those cases has seen the output. That is a warm, credible list nobody else can assemble, and it is the fastest path to a second and third customer.

The demo, as the whole sales motion. Nothing in this market is won on a feature list. It is won by loading a real extraction and a real disclosure bundle in front of a sceptical investigator and asking a question that spans both. Every competitive claim in this document is demonstrable in ten minutes; none of it is provable in a deck.

What to charge

Per matter, in the $6,000–24,000 band, benchmarked against investigator hours rather than against software. The reasoning is in the pricing section, but the short version is that per-seat pricing misprices a practice with four large matters and a hundred small ones, and per-gigabyte pricing — the industry default — actively pays the customer to keep evidence out of the system, which is incoherent for a product whose value grows with how much of the case it holds.

The comparison to make is the one the lead prospect made unprompted: the cost of a junior investigator or overseas review staff. That framing puts Loupe in a budget line that already exists and is already large, and it sidesteps a direct price fight with a free tier that cannot be undercut.

What to lead with

Not citations — they are free everywhere. Not "AI for criminal defence" — four funded companies already own that sentence. Lead with the thing that is demonstrably true and structurally hard to copy: a question put to the whole case at once, answered from a model built out of the phone extraction, the documents and the financial records together, with every claim resolving back to the artifact it came from. Then show a call, a transfer and a line of a police report sitting in the same timeline, and let the investigator ask what happens next.

What has to be fixed first

SOC 2 Type II. Matey — the closest direct competitor, selling to the same buyer — holds SOC 2 Type II and ISO 27001 today. Longeye holds SOC 2 Type II and runs single-tenant on GovCloud. Loupe holds neither. In a segment handling privileged material under protective orders this is the first question a firm's counsel asks, and it gates the pilot rather than merely slowing it. It is also the cheapest gap on the list to close, and nothing else on this page matters until it is.

Second, the two capabilities this document leans on that are not yet shipped — Loupes and the disconfirmation agent — need to demo clean once before they appear in any external material. The existing discipline is the right one: build first, claim second, and anything unshipped on submission day becomes the roadmap answer instead.

The sequence, in one line

Get SOC 2. Win the defence bar through NACDL and one state association. Convert the casework referrals already available. Price per matter against investigator hours. Then, with references and a certification in hand, move to corporate internal investigations — where the same product carries a larger contract and phone data stops being the point.

05 — Findings

Eleven things the research settled

Each of these changes a decision. They are ordered by how much they should change.

F1

Citations became a giveaway — so the argument has to move underneath them

Between November 2025 and February 2026 the eDiscovery majors did something unusual: they shipped their best AI features and then stopped charging for them. Relativity folded aiR for Review and Privilege into the base RelativityOne rate. Everlaw bundled Writing Assistant, Deposition Analyzer and single-document Review Assistant into core per-gigabyte pricing and cut batch coding more than 40%. DISCO announced the industry's first scaled agentic AI at no additional cost. Reveal gave aji away to every legal team through the end of 2025.

The quality is real, too. This is not marketing veneer over a thin retrieval layer: Relativity's aiR will not emit a fact it cannot match to source text, and Everlaw's Deep Dive was tested on databases in the tens of millions of documents and returns "insufficient evidence" rather than inventing one. Meanwhile NotebookLM has taught every lawyer alive to expect a clickable citation for free.

So — Loupe cannot lead with cited answers, and should not try. The market expects them and the incumbents give them away. Provenance is a qualifying condition. The wedge is one layer down: what the answer is computed over.
F2

Every competitor answers by retrieval; none answers by traversal

This is the finding the product argument should rest on, and it is verifiable from the vendors' own documentation rather than from their marketing. Across all forty-seven platforms the mechanism is identical: retrieve candidate passages, place them in a context window, ask a model to reason over them. The differences are in retrieval quality, not in kind.

The consequence is that answer completeness is bounded by the retriever, and every vendor who documents this honestly says so. Reveal states plainly that Ask is "designed for precision, not recall" — it surfaces the top hundred semantically similar sources and cannot find all instances of anything. Relativity's conversational layer caps at 300,000 documents per index and 1.5M per workspace, and its fact extraction runs 5,000 documents per job. CoCounsel manages roughly 200 documents per comparison run with quality explicitly degrading at higher counts, and 2,000 pages per document. Harvey's vaults run a prompt per document across up to 10,000 files and aggregate — parallel document-at-a-time extraction rather than a query. Everlaw's Deep Dive is the strongest of them, genuinely reaching the whole corpus with confidence ranking, and it is still retrieval plus reasoning over documents: it cannot answer a question whose answer is a relationship, because relationships are not modelled.

A graph changes what a question is. Resolve the evidence into people, devices, accounts, events and transfers, with the relationships between them and a source on every node, and "who contacted this person in the fortnight before that transfer" stops being a search and becomes a traversal with a definite answer over everything ingested. Nothing is sampled, so nothing is silently missed, and the citation is a property of the structure rather than something the model was asked to attach. The graph vendors have this and cannot get evidence into it; the legal vendors have the evidence and no graph. That intersection is the product.

So — the sentence is not "we cite our answers." It is "we can put a question to the whole case at once and the answer is grounded in a model of it, not assembled by a language model reading documents one at a time." That is demonstrable in ten minutes on a real case, and no competitor can run the same demo.
F3

The category exists to make a pile smaller. Investigation exists to build an account up.

This is the deepest difference in the document, and it explains several of the others. eDiscovery is a reductive discipline. Its entire apparatus — early case assessment, culling, deduplication, technology-assisted review, threading, per-gigabyte pricing — exists to take an enormous corpus and make it small enough that humans can read what remains. The verb is reduce, and vendors say so openly: Nuix markets its enrichment as cutting review volumes 40–60%. Success is defined as fewer documents to look at.

Investigation runs the opposite way. Nobody building a defence theory is trying to read less; they are trying to assemble an account of what happened. Every artifact processed should make the model of the case richer, not shorter. The unit of progress is not "documents remaining in the review queue" — it is "what do we now know, what connects to what, and what still doesn't fit." Search and reduce leaves you with a smaller pile. It does not leave you with a case.

This reframes the phone-data finding as a consequence rather than an oversight. If the job is to cull toward a reviewable set, a call log is noise — it isn't document-shaped, nobody is going to read it, so flattening it into a spreadsheet is a rational thing to do. If the job is to build a model, that same call log is connective tissue: it is precisely the structure that links a person to a device to a moment in time. The majors are not failing at phone data because they lack engineering. They are discarding it because their paradigm has no place to put it, and their pricing rewards having less of it.

The constructive layers that do exist are bolted onto reductive platforms and inherit the limits. Everlaw's Storybuilder, DISCO's Timelines, Relativity's aiR for Case Strategy and Casefleet's approved-facts model are all real, all good, and all document-scoped — you build up a chronology out of what survived the culling, from the one evidence type the platform models properly.

So — the workspace framing is not a UI preference, it is the product's thesis: a case is built, and the platform should accumulate structure as evidence arrives rather than filter it away. It also argues directly for per-matter pricing — per-gigabyte pricing charges by volume and pays you to keep evidence out.
F4

Everyone transcribes. Nobody turns what was said into evidence you can query.

Be precise here, because the weak version of this claim is easy to disprove. Audio and video transcription is close to universal in 2026 and is not a differentiator on its own. JusticeText is built on it. Longeye does real-time transcription and translation. Matey transcribes during ingestion. Harvey accepts audio and returns transcripts with speaker labels and timestamps. Hebbia bulk-transcribes a hundred files at a time across sixty languages. Casepoint added transcription in March 2026, Everlaw and Casefleet meter it, Cellebrite's Guardian summarises audio threads, and Cognyte analyses speech down to accent and affect. Anyone claiming transcription as a moat will be corrected in the first demo.

What happens next is where every one of them stops. In each of those products the transcript becomes a document — searchable text, sitting beside other searchable text. The name spoken in a jail call at 02:14 is a string in a file. It is not the same object as that person's name on a bank statement, a contact record in a phone extraction, or a line in a police report, and no question can traverse between them.

Loupe extracts entities out of transcripts, video and images and resolves them into the same case graph as everything else. A person named aloud in a recorded call becomes the same node as the person who received the transfer and the person in the contact book — one identity, several sources, each citable back to the artifact and the timestamp it came from. That makes a spoken sentence a first-class piece of evidence rather than an attachment, and it makes cross-modal questions answerable: who is mentioned in these calls who also appears in the financial records but never in the disclosure?

That nobody else does this is not a gap in effort — it follows from the structure already established. The legal and forensic tools that transcribe well have no graph to put entities in. The graph platforms that could hold them have no ingestion pipeline for audio and video. The capability requires both halves at once, and the two halves currently live in different companies.

So — never sell transcription. Sell what transcription becomes: a name spoken in a call and a name written in a statement are the same entity in the case model, and a single question reaches both.
F5

Configuration happens at review time everywhere else; Loupe configures the front door

A quieter difference, and one that only shows up when a real case arrives. Competitors let you tune how AI reviews material that is already loaded: Relativity's no-code custom analyses for aiR, Reveal's AI Model Library of reusable models transferable across matters, Everlaw's custom extractions, Harvey's Agent Builder and five hundred prebuilt agents, Nuix's AI-tuned solution packs. All of it operates after ingestion, on a corpus whose shape is already fixed.

What eDiscovery calls a "processing profile" is a technical artifact, not an analytical one — deduplication, OCR settings, time zone, container expansion, what to do with embedded files. It governs how bytes are unpacked, not what the system understands them to mean.

Loupe's AI processing profiles sit at the user's ingestion step and govern extraction itself: which entity and event types matter for this matter, how this document family should be read, what a financial record means here versus in a different case. Because the model is built as evidence arrives rather than assembled afterwards, the configuration has to happen at the front door — and it means every file type and data type is handled on the way in rather than normalised into the lowest common denominator of "reviewable document."

So — this is the mechanism behind the two findings above. You can only traverse a model you actually built, and you only get a model worth traversing if extraction was directed at ingestion. It is also the honest answer to "couldn't you just point an LLM at the files?" — you could, and you would get a pile of text.
F6

Phone evidence is the category's structural blind spot, and it is not closing

This is the finding that matters most, because it is the one nobody is fixing. Where phone support exists at all in the legal stack, only chats survive as reviewable objects — and even those get chopped into artificial units. Everlaw splits conversations every thousand messages. Reveal slices them into 24-hour blocks, each block becoming "a document."

Everything else is discarded into a spreadsheet. Relativity's own processing documentation is explicit: calls, contacts, calendar, locations, web history and installed apps are flattened into Excel files in an "Other Data" folder, call logs emerge "only as call log data in Excel format, not as individual records," voicemail recordings are unsupported, and multi-device extractions are unsupported outright. DISCO and Casepoint show no Cellebrite documentation at all. i2 removed native UFED import at version 9.1.0 and now instructs customers to export CSV and hand-write an import spec.

The forensics vendors model devices beautifully and documents barely. Cellebrite Pathfinder and Guardian own the extraction and are weak on discovery corpora; Guardian's launch release claims documents, images, mobile data and CDRs but not audio transcription, financial records or email. Magnet unifies mobile, cloud, computer, drone and vehicle — five kinds of device, no kinds of document.

So — the sentence to own is not "we handle phone data." It is "a call at 02:14 and a wire transfer and a paragraph of a police report are the same kind of citable object here." No competitor in the set can say it.
F7

The defence cannot buy the analytics the prosecution runs on

This asymmetry is documentable, which makes it usable. GrayKey is explicitly restricted: available to law enforcement, public safety and defence agencies, with agency-email vetting, and Magnet states plainly that it is not available to the private sector. Cellebrite Pathfinder — the analytics layer, not the extraction tool — is marketed solely to law enforcement, government, intelligence and corporate investigators; no defence sales channel surfaced anywhere in the research.

The sharpest artifact found is a Florida State Attorney's office publishing instructions for defence counsel on how to receive phone extractions through read-only Guardian share links. The defence reviews the prosecution's evidence inside the prosecution's tool, with the prosecution's permissions.

What defence teams can buy — Magnet Axiom, Exterro FTK, Cellebrite Physical Analyzer — is device-by-device examiner tooling with no cross-evidence analytical model. Siren has genuine native Cellebrite connectors, and gates them to "qualifying organisations."

So — "levelling the evidentiary playing field" is not a slogan here, it is a description of a licensing structure. It should be said plainly and sourced.

F9

The two halves of the answer sit in different companies

Entity resolution across Relativity, Everlaw and Reveal is, without exception, email-header alias consolidation: Name Normalization parses headers into Entity records; Everlaw's Entity Management groups contact addresses by shared name words; Brainspace merges nodes manually. Communication analysis exists at all three and is email-only — it does not span chat or phone identities. Harvey, Hebbia, CoCounsel, Clearbrief and CaseMap build no entity model at all.

The graph vendors have the opposite problem. Linkurious is a front end that requires an already-populated graph database; its only native ingestion is a CSV import added in 2026. Maltego's graph is an analyst's working canvas fed by outbound OSINT queries, with no path for received discovery. Octostar's virtual graph over ClickHouse is explicitly designed to avoid ETL — it federates databases an agency already owns, which is the exact inverse of a defence case, where nothing is modelled until you model it.

So — the intersection of graph-native and pipeline-native is genuinely unoccupied, and it is exactly what whole-corpus querying requires: you need both a model to traverse and a way to build it from the evidence as received. An architecture claim — a statement about what the system is built on rather than what features it has. A feature claim ("we have a timeline view") can be copied in a sprint and cannot be proved in a meeting; an architecture claim can be tested live in ten minutes and takes a competitor eighteen months to close.
F10

Durable narrative objects exist — but none of them can hold a phone call

The "Loupes" concept is less unique than it looked, and the precise version of it is more unique than it looked. The sharpest example is Comtrac, whose "Elementising Evidence" methodology maps exhibits directly to the elements of an offence and auto-populates an investigation matrix with what must be proven — the closest shipped analogue anywhere to a curated, evidenced collection assembled to establish something. It is exhibit-scoped and cannot hold a call or a transfer. Everlaw's Storybuilder Fact Management, shipped December 2025, makes facts first-class objects linked to evidence, people, depositions and drafts — and it is now bundled free. DISCO Timelines persist from case opening to trial with facts dragged straight from the document viewer. Relativity's aiR for Case Strategy classifies facts as helpful or harmful, arranges them in issue swim lanes and flags gaps. Casefleet has the best human-in-the-loop pattern in the entire set: AI proposes facts, and they enter the record only after an attorney approves them. CaseMap has had facts, issues and objects since 1998.

Every one of them is document-scoped. None can cite a phone event as a timeline fact, because none models one. The differentiated claim is not the container — it is what is allowed inside it.

So — stop describing Loupes as a novel object. Describe them as the only evidence collection that can contain a call, a transfer and a document line at once.
F11

Disconfirmation is unclaimed — but narrow versions exist, so the claim has to be precise

No platform in the set markets contradiction detection or disconfirmation as a headline capability, and that remains true — but narrow versions of the capability do exist, and the claim has to be stated precisely enough to survive a demo. JusticeText's Miranda AI identifies inconsistencies across evidence and Clearbrief's cite-checking catches fabricated citations. Both are narrow: Miranda compares across A/V discovery, Clearbrief checks citations inside your own filing. Neither runs disconfirmation over a structured claims ledger — and the ledger is the difference between flagging an inconsistency and enumerating what would break a theory, including the document that does not exist. The nearest approaches are narrow: Relativity's helpful-versus-harmful fact classification and gap-spotting; NexLaw ChronoVault flagging conflicting testimony and timeline gaps; JusticeText's Miranda AI identifying inconsistencies across evidence; Siren's K9 flagging its own uncertainty; Clearbrief's cite-checking, which catches fabricated citations in your own filing. Octostar used the word "contradictions" once, on a conference booth sign, with no product behind it.

This also happens to be the answer to the strongest objection the hypothesis framing invites. An investigator who states a theory and has a machine populate it is doing confirmation bias at speed, and opposing counsel will say exactly that. A tool that systematically looks for what breaks the theory is not a disclaimer bolted onto that risk — it is the inversion of it.

So — build it. It is the only capability in this document that no competitor has claimed, it answers the sharpest question about the product, and it requires the claims ledger that the provenance model already implies.
06 — Whitespace

What is actually unoccupied

Separating the genuinely empty from the merely crowded-but-winnable, because the two demand different strategies.

Genuinely empty. Four things: whole-corpus questions answered by traversing a model rather than retrieving passages; a case model that holds phone, financial and document evidence as one citable object set; disconfirmation as a first-class capability; and narrative collections whose members can be events rather than only documents. Nothing in the research contradicts any of these, and the first three were searched for specifically.

Crowded but winnable. Defence-side AI discovery has four funded entrants, none of which has a graph or a real device model. The competition is for the category label, not the capability — and the capability is where a pilot is won or lost once a firm actually loads a case.

Available but shallow. Audio and video transcription is now close to universal and should not be sold as a differentiator on its own; what is rare is transcript content landing in the same model as everything else, so a spoken sentence is as citable and as connectable as a line in a bank statement. Constructive workspaces exist too — Storybuilder, DISCO Timelines, Casefleet, CaseMap — but every one of them is a curation layer over a reductive document platform. The difference is not that the feature is missing; it is that the platform underneath is built to subtract.

Closed. Cited document Q&A — note the qualifier, document — chronology generation from documents, and per-seat legal AI assistants. These are done, they are free or nearly free, and entering them means competing with Relativity's distribution and Harvey's balance sheet.

The gap nobody is defending. Small and mid-sized investigative practices are structurally underserved by every incumbent, and the reason is economic rather than technical. Harvey prices for the AmLaw 100 with seat minimums that put entry near $360,000 a year. Relativity sells small firms exclusively through a partner channel. DataWalk starts around $560,000. Palantir averages over a million per deal. Casepoint sells to federal agencies. The products a small practice can actually authorise are workflow tools, not analysis. CROSStrax runs $35 a month and Trackops $99 a month, both built specifically for private investigators — the exact beachhead buyer — and both sit at the very bottom of the study on evidence modelling. i2 Analyst's Notebook at roughly $7,160 a year is a manual charting tool with no LLM in any tier, and Logikcull's pay-as-you-go tier is document hosting. The buyer already owns cheap case management and owns nothing that analyses evidence.

The one to watch — a competitor's strategy, not ours

To be unambiguous: giving Loupe away to public defenders is not the plan, and this section is the reason we do not enter that market first. The free tiers described here belong to rivals. Everlaw for Good is free, covers CJA panel attorneys explicitly, and has passed $6M in donated technology across 235–300 organisations. It is charity-funded, which caps how far it can scale — but it means a federal defence attorney can already get a serious platform for nothing. Any pricing conversation in this segment happens in that shadow.

What this implies for Loupe's model

Price per matter, not per seat — and note that per-gigabyte pricing is not neutral. It charges by evidence volume, which means it pays the customer to keep evidence out of the platform. That is coherent for a discipline whose goal is culling and incoherent for one whose goal is building a complete account. Four arguments converge on per-matter. 08a — Market sizing

What each of these markets is actually worth

Fit decides who wins a buyer; it says nothing about how many buyers exist. Every market in the study sized against published analyst research, so that a strong fit in a small market is not mistaken for an opportunity. Ranges are given where research houses disagree — on several of these categories they differ by two to three times depending on whether services are counted alongside software and how the category is drawn.

The field splits into two halves that never meet, and the buyer sits in the gap between them.

One half can model evidence. Cellebrite, Magnet and Palantir build real structure out of what they ingest — and either they will not sell to a defence practice at all, or what they model is devices and nothing else. Magnet unifies five kinds of hardware and no kinds of document. Pathfinder builds a genuine graph and is marketed solely to law enforcement, government and intelligence.

The other half serves the buyer. Relativity, Everlaw, DISCO, Reveal, Harvey, CoCounsel — all purchasable, all excellent at documents, and all of them treating a phone extraction as an attachment. Relativity's own documentation says call logs emerge "only as call log data in Excel format, not as individual records," and that multi-device extractions are unsupported outright.

Forty-seven platforms, and the intersection is empty. That is not an artifact of how the axes were drawn — it is structural. Modelling evidence and serving this buyer are different engineering problems sold to different customers, and the companies that solved one have no commercial reason to solve the other. Four funded startups entered the defence-AI category in eighteen months, which settles whether the buyer spends. None of them has a graph.

The gap is not that nobody is good enough. It is that the capability and the customer live in different companies.

One more thing this explains. The team went looking for a platform to run live federal casework, tried a number of them, and none could work the case. Building was the second choice, not the first — the structural finding arriving as lived experience rather than as analysis. A buyer in this position evaluates the market and finds that everything able to model the evidence will not sell to them, and everything that will sell to them treats a phone extraction as an attachment. Trying several and finding none fit is the predicted outcome, not bad luck.

On cost, the finding is simpler and more useful: this budget is already being spent.

A defence team does not choose between Loupe and a competitor. It chooses between Loupe and what it does today — pay a forensic vendor to extract each device, pay per gigabyte per month to host the result somewhere it cannot be properly analysed, pay an examiner by the hour to look at parts of it, then pay its own people to stitch the pieces together by hand.

On a serious matter that runs to $34,000–$131,000 and sixty to a hundred hours before anyone has answered a question about the evidence — and the largest line in it, hosting, is charged per gigabyte per month, so it grows with exactly the material this platform exists to model. A 435GB federal case costs $4,350 a month to keep hosted. The outcome that money buys is a spreadsheet.

So the sale is not "find budget for this." It is "you are already paying for this, and here is what you are getting for it." That is a far easier conversation, and it is why the absence of an analyst category for defence-side investigation software does not indicate an absent market. The spend is real, recurring and per-matter. It flows to vendors rather than to software — which is what a category looks like immediately before it forms.

The one line that does not move is extraction: devices still go out at $3,650 a case. Everything downstream of it does. Between 48,000 and 125,000 evidence-heavy private criminal-defence matters run in the US each year carrying $1.0–7.5B of displaceable spend, and capturing a fifth of it across one to five thousand firms is a $350M–$900M business — before any adjacent market.

The beachhead has no software category, so the size that matters is the spend already committed per matter. Three lines exist today in every phone-heavy defence matter, and only one of them survives.

Retained — extraction. Devices still go out to a forensic vendor: $1,575–2,975 per phone, $3,650 flat for a one-or-two-device case, $600 per device beyond. Loupe analyses what was produced rather than acquiring it, so this line is untouched and is never claimed as a saving.

Displaced — processing, hosting and examiner analysis. eDiscovery processing runs $3–10 per gigabyte and hosting $5–15 per gigabyte per month, charged for the life of the matter; examiner time for analysis rather than extraction runs $300–500 an hour. Phone extractions are the largest artifacts in the case, so this is where the money goes — and it is spent to have calls, contacts and locations flattened into spreadsheets.

The regional plan

Three jurisdictions, three different jobs — sequenced by what each one is for, not by size.

Ireland — the reference, not the revenue

€123M of criminal legal aid and a €215M Courts Service is not a market to build a company on, and should never be presented as one. Ireland's job is to produce the first nameable customer in the company's own jurisdiction. The buyer is criminal defence practices doing Circuit and Central Criminal Court work and the firms on the Criminal Legal Aid panel; the route is the Law Society and that panel, which is the same bar-association channel that has already been proven in the US. No gate bites — EU data residency is trivially satisfied by single-tenant deployment. Fee restoration in 2026 means the segment's budget is rising.

United Kingdom — the first real European revenue

21.76% of the European digital-forensics market, roughly $525M, and the clearest funded pain of any jurisdiction here. The buyer is criminal defence solicitors running Crown Court work — the same shape as the US beachhead. The tailwind is a government problem with money attached: ~80,200 open Crown Court cases, projected to 99,000–114,000 by March 2029, with £92M added to criminal legal aid in July 2025 and fees raised that December. The route is the Law Society and the Criminal Law Solicitors' Association, mirroring NACDL.

Defence first, policing much later. In the UK/EU policing profile, disclosure and production mechanics are gated at 8 under CPIA and Loupe scores 2 — the heaviest single gap anywhere in this study for that buyer. Defence work does not carry the disclosure duty the same way, which is exactly why it is the entry point and prosecution is not. BlueLight Commercial's role as a policing procurement gatekeeper remains unconfirmed and should be established before any public-sector effort is planned around it.

Europe — expansion, on a structural advantage

Digital forensics $2.41B (2026) → $3.89B by 2031; legal technology $6.81B → $15.45B by 2034, with Europe at 25% of the global legal-tech market. The advantage is deployment — single-tenant, in-jurisdiction, sovereign, scored 9 against 2–6 for most American-owned competitors, and under GDPR that is a procurement requirement rather than a preference. The obstacle is fragmentation: every jurisdiction has its own procedure, disclosure regime, languages and vendor rate card, and there is no European equivalent of the US state-court dataset. Treat Europe beyond Ireland and the UK as partner-led, and never carry a single European TAM figure into an investor conversation as though it were addressable in one go.

The sequence. Ireland produces the reference. The UK produces the first European revenue and the strongest policy tailwind in the study. The US remains the largest opportunity and the one the product was built inside. Europe beyond those two is later and partner-led, and its value is the deployment advantage rather than any single national market.

The framing that follows from this

The first market is a services-substitution play into a pool worth more than $15B in the United States alone, entered at a price a practice signs off without a procurement process. The sized software categories — investigation management at $8.1B growing 14.8%, insurance fraud detection at $8.5–11.3B growing 23.2% — are the second and third moves, once references and certifications exist.

One limit, stated plainly. The displacement argument holds for firms that already pay a vendor to process and host. A share of small defence practices receive a hard drive and never host anything — for them there is no invoice to displace, and the value is labour and capability rather than substituted spend. That determines which half of the pitch lands: cheaper than what you pay now for the hosting firms, you can finally do this at all for the rest.

Where Loupe is weakest

Written to be uncomfortable, because an investor's diligence will find all of these and it is better to have an answer ready than a surprise.

No certifications

Matey holds SOC 2 Type II and ISO 27001. Harvey, Hebbia, Clearbrief, Casefleet and Supio hold SOC 2 Type II. Everlaw and Relativity hold FedRAMP; Casepoint holds FedRAMP High with DOD IL5 and IL6. No published scale proof

Everlaw tested Deep Dive across databases in the tens of millions of documents. Relativity processed 8.7 petabytes in a year. Nuix ingests 1,000+ file types. Loupe has federal matters that went to trial — which is better evidence of value but weaker evidence of scale, and diligence asks about both.

No ecosystem integrations

No iManage or NetDocuments, no Clio or MyCase, no Westlaw or Lexis. CoCounsel, Clearbrief and Litera all have them, and for a law firm the document management system is where the work already lives. Every integration absent is a manual step a competitor has removed.

Dependent on what was produced

Loupe ingests extraction output; it does not acquire. Cellebrite, Magnet and Exterro own acquisition through analysis. On the defence side that is largely fine — the evidence arrives via discovery — but it means Loupe can never see what the extraction did not capture, and cannot independently verify the acquisition.

Two headline capabilities are not shipped

Loupes are in build and uncommitted. The disconfirmation agent is a design. Both appear in positioning material. The discipline the NDRC pack already imposes is the right one: nothing enters a deck until it has demoed clean once, and anything unshipped on submission day becomes the roadmap answer instead.

Funding asymmetry

Harvey has raised over $1B at an $11B valuation. Matey raised $7.5M for the same buyer; TrialKit $4.25M; Longeye $5M from a16z. Loupe is pre-seed. The compensating advantage is that the product was built inside live federal casework rather than from a market hypothesis — which is real, and which needs to be said early because the balance sheet cannot be.

Neo4j's Aura Agent builds GraphRAG agents with no code and exposes them over MCP. A competent two-to-four person team reaches "cited Q&A over a document corpus with an entity graph" in months, not eighteen.

What the production literature lists as absent is precise and useful: entity-resolution quality control, ontology maintenance, retrieval monitoring, and native provenance tracking or audit trails. None of these frameworks ships per-claim citation pinned to source bytes, chain-of-custody metadata, forensic-format ingestion, curation objects, reviewer workflow or defensible export. They are retrieval libraries, not evidence systems. Worth noting: Microsoft announced LazyGraphRAG in November 2024, promised open-source integration as "the next milestone," and still had not shipped it as of July 2026.

Frontier labsClaude for Legal · ChatGPT Projects · Gemini Enterprise · NotebookLM Corpus limits are the wall — and MCP is the real strategic event

The scale limits are decisive. ChatGPT Projects caps at 40 files. Claude Projects switch to retrieval above ~150K tokens for roughly 1.5M tokens of project knowledge. NotebookLM tops out at 500–600 sources, each capped at 500,000 words. A single phone extraction can be tens of gigabytes and hundreds of thousands of artifacts. None of them ingests a UFDR, models a device, or produces a chain-of-custody record — and none offers customer-premises deployment, though NotebookLM Enterprise runs inside the customer's own GCP project, which narrows the confidentiality argument for cloud-tolerant buyers.

The strategic event is not capability, it is MCP. Claude for Legal launched in May 2026 with a litigation plugin and connectors into Relativity, Everlaw and Consilio; Everlaw shipped its own Anthropic integration; Thomson Reuters rebuilt CoCounsel on the Claude Agent SDK. The answer is not to compete with the agent layer but to be the repository worth connecting to — the system of record for evidence, provenance and curation.

Method, confidence and what is still unverified

Compiled from seventeen research sweeps run 25–26 July 2026, drawing on more than 350 sourced URLs. The first pass was an adversarial workflow: 107 agents across five angles extracted 118 claims from 25 fetched sources, of which 25 were put to three independent skeptics each — 17 confirmed, 3 refuted and killed, 5 left mid-vote when the run hit its quota. The remaining sweeps were per-vendor deep reads with a standing instruction to flag anything unverifiable.

Three claims were refuted and are excluded from this document: that Nuix discloses no provenance mechanism, that Guardian Investigate leaves defence-side segments unaddressed as originally framed, and that Guardian Investigate was still in design-partner testing — it went generally available in March 2026.

Two research premises turned out to be wrong and are corrected here: Litera does not own CaseMap (LexisNexis does, and Litera exited litigation fact management in 2024), and Casepoint has no platform called "Aurora" — that is Consilio's, and it runs on Reveal.

Still unverified, and flagged before external use: the Matey funding and traction details were mid-verification when the first run hit its quota; BlueLight Commercial's role as the UK policing procurement gatekeeper is unconfirmed; proposed Federal Rule of Evidence 707 on machine-generated evidence is from background knowledge and was never sourced; Cellebrite's formal policy on selling analytics to defence firms is strongly indicated by its marketing but not documented either way; and most seat-price figures for Harvey, Hebbia, CoCounsel and Everlaw are third-party estimates rather than published rates.

A second verification pass on 26 July re-checked multimedia handling, ingestion-time configuration and the reductive-versus-constructive paradigm across twenty-one platforms. It produced several corrections now folded in — DISCO does transcribe with diarization at ingest, Relativity's diarization labels are internal and unsearchable, Harvey handles audio but no documented video and publishes no duration cap, Siren has no multimedia capability at all, and Cellebrite states in its own materials that Genesis maintains no persistent case model. That pass exhausted the session's search budget, so a handful of items remain unverified and are listed as such below.

Two entries are corrected against a primary source stronger than the web. Siren's Cellebrite integration and Octostar's phone-analysis capability are both recorded per Neil's first-hand knowledge, overriding a vendor marketing page in the first case and an absence of any public documentation in the second. Where founder knowledge and a vendor page conflict, this document follows the founder knowledge and says so — but note that such claims are unciteable externally, so they inform strategy rather than appearing in investor material.

Where an entry says "no public evidence," the capability was searched for in product pages, documentation, release notes and press coverage and not found. For several vendors that is absence of evidence rather than proven absence, and the per-vendor dossiers say which.
